# A Christian Anthropology for Intelligence Amplification

**Unit:** TH790 Research Project: Theology  
**Date submitted:** 28/2/2017  
**Disclaimer:** The original manuscript was submitted 28/2/2017. Minor edits were included prior to uploading for public consumption in November 2026.

## Abstract

This research project proposes a Christian Anthropology for Intelligence Amplification (IA - also known as "cognitive augmentation" or "machine augmented intelligence").

Human beings, it is argued, are created as technologically self-transformative and, when ontologically restored in union with Christ, are uniquely suited for self-extension through Intelligence Amplification.

The anthropology is modelled around three "Limits-Pairs" that find their apex in Christ. Together these "limits" form boundaries and trajectories for answering what it means to be human where information technology is increasingly used to augment and extend human thinking.

Limit-Pair One (LP1) - "human embodiment and transformation" - lays the foundations to define humans as technologically self-transformative. Limit-Pair Two (LP2) - "human individuality and relationality" - argues that individual identities are relationally restored and fully realised through their "union with Christ;" and the ongoing outworking (by the Spirit) in "communion" encompasses IA technology. Limit-Pair Three (LP3) - "human agency and automation" - concludes that, as IA automation accelerates towards union with Artificial Intelligence, those already in union with Christ are uniquely suited to "maintain" human agency, and indeed "maximise" Christocentric agency in IA.

## Introduction

This research project seeks to contribute a Christian Anthropology to the growing discourse on "Intelligence Amplification" (IA) - what does it mean to be human where, increasingly, our thinking is done by machines.

The study of IA, under the banner of Cognitive Science, is the 'interdisciplinary study of mind that draws upon psychology, computer science, neuroscience, philosophy, linguistics and the social sciences,' where each of these fields build upon their own (sometimes implicit) anthropological presuppositions.[^1] This research project eschews attempts to synthesize these varying schools, opting instead to contribute an anthropological model that is distinctly Christian in its orientation. This more confined Christian perspective offers an alternate, and under-represented, anthropological paradigm in IA; a paradigm that draws upon established and deep epistemological roots in theology and history.[^2]

Moreover, the subject for this study emphasises theology over ethics. While this distinction is somewhat fluid and artificial, the framing of this research seeks to begin with underlying questions concerning human nature - "when is an IA human no longer considered human?" or "can IA make us more human?" - in order to construct normative frameworks for better ethical discussions regarding whether we "should or should not" proceed with IA enhancements. Brian Edgar's appeal for a "biotheology" to provide greater form and depth to bioethical discussions offers a helpful parallel for this work, which could be seen as an early precursor to a fuller "cybertheology."[^3] Indeed in studying IA, such a cybertheology (or cyber-anthropology) holds urgency as humanity enters an era where questions like "what is the meaning of life?" quickly become practical engineering questions ahead of philosophical and ethical ones.[^4]

The project will be structured as follows: after a brief definition and scoping of IA for the project, the anthropological model is presented - a three part "Limit-Pair" Anthropology, where each pair features an immanence limit and a transcendence limit that, together, address how to theologically consider humans that are technologically augmented with machine thinking. The bulk of the project unpacks each Limit-Pair, presenting first the argument and biblical-theological evidence for each limit, followed by a brief exploration of how the limits might be theologically paired in practice.

## Defining "Intelligence Amplification"

### Touching on Transhumanism

This project's anthropology overlaps with (but re-evaluates) the wider field of "Transhumanism," which is defined here as a multi-faceted movement that aims to transform the human condition through technology to enhance human capacities; transformation beyond "natural" human capacities towards a "post human" state.[^5] The scope of Transhumanism is cross-disciplinary and all-encompassing - e.g. intellectual, physical, and psychological - and its visions for the future are diverse (and often contradictory).

This project, therefore, reduces the scope to overlap with one particular area of Transhumanism - that of "Intelligence Amplification," which is defined as: the use of information technology to augment and extend human cognition. The synonymous terms "cognitive augmentation," or "machine augmented intelligence" will also be used through the project for varying emphasis.

### Distinct from Artificial Intelligence

Briefly, Intelligence Amplification ("I" before "A") is related to, but distinct from "Artificial Intelligence" (AI, "A" before "I") - the more common term that describes autonomous machine intelligence. The distinction is important. For while IA seeks to extend the human "self," AI seeks the creation of an "other" an intelligence independent from the human.[^6]

So, tracing the history between humans and computers, John Markoff contrasts the very different research philosophies and origins between AI and IA.

> During the past half century, McCarthy's [AI] and Engelbart's [IA] philosophies have remained separate and their central conflict strands unresolved. One approach supplants humans with an increasingly powerful blend of computer hardware and software. The other extends our reach intellectually, economically, and socially using the same ingredients.[^7]

This follows Jeanine Thweatt-Bates' caution that theologians must clarify the stream of Transhumanism being addressed, lest the theological responses be misdirected amidst Transhumanism's diversity.

There are, however, signs of convergence. Today's early "smart" AI algorithms are fast becoming the consumer (in)visible face of Intelligence Amplification - a "soft" self-extension through AI personal assistants (Apple Siri, Google Assistant, Microsoft Cortana, Amazon Alexa), recommendation engines (Netflix, YouTube media, Amazon shopping), and content curation (Facebook social and news feeds, Google search results). Though this project limits its scope IA, sometimes IA uses AI.[^8]

### Does Not need Wires

Finally, then, while society is familiar with pacemakers and prosthetic limbs, there exists nothing (yet) that approaches a "bionic brain." So, it might be tempting to dismiss an "anthropology for IA" as a highly premature and speculative theological exercise towards a distant hypothetical future.

Andy Clark is helpful here in his "cultural" definition of the "cyborg," a broader interpretation of a cyborg 'not in the merely superficial sense of combining flesh and wires but in the more profound sense of pre-existent human-technology symbionts: thinking and reasoning systems whose minds and selves are spread across biological brain and nonbiological circuitry.'[^9] That is, our use of cognitive technology - specifically, "information technology" - can be seen as an existing hybridisation between mind and machine; we are already immersed in and a part of the complex network of social media, mobile technologies and the internet. Moreover, unlike physical augmentation, cognitive augmentation is invisible but pervasive. Humans already interact with machines with a fluidity that is similar in essence to more direct computer-brain interfaces; the difference between today's "internet-connected smartphone," and near-future "neural lace" is primarily one of bandwidth.

The term "cyborg" then is intentionally provocative to drive home a point: that what is more apparent today - that technology extends us and shapes us - has always been true. As culturally defined, the "information-cyborg" is not a modern development, but stretches through a long history of cognitive technologies back to the fundamentals of communication itself - the spoken and written word.[^10] If *homo sapiens* are, in a sense, *techno sapiens* - perhaps it is not primarily in their physical tool-making, but in the mental mediation and enhancement through technology.[^11] This anthropological model hopes to reframe pressing wisdom questions around whether and how we engage with technology moving forward.

## Model Summary

The proposed anthropological model is structured around three "Limit-Pairs" that pair together dual Christian imperatives of immanence and transcendence. The definition of a "Limit-Pair" is more strictly defined in the following Model Design section. Firstly, an overview of the anthropological dimensions is presented here:

### Limit-Pair One (LP1): Embodiment and Transformation

The first limit-pairs is labelled "Embodiment and Transformation." On the one hand, IA must not reduce a human's intrinsic embodied nature. Specifically, humans must continue to have bodies and minds and those must remain mutually dependent. On the other hand, an embodiment that is not "flesh and blood" finds compatibility with Christian anthropology, and moreover there is warrant in seeing transformative technologies as inherent in Scripture's teleological metanarrative - humans are self-transformational technological beings.

However, contrary to typical Transhumanist pursuit of reason as the ultimate (or at least instrumental) motivation, the suggested approach to pair these limits is through doctrines of grace and mercy - an atypical and counter-cultural inversion of strength and weakness, as exemplified by Christ.

### Limit-Pair Two (LP2): Individuality and Relationality

The second limit-pair is labelled "Individuality and Relationality". On the one hand, IA must not reduce a human's individuality in the twin sense that we are unitary beings that must remain singular and distinctive. On the other hand, humans are relational beings where our common information technology already network together human minds; IA accelerates and more tightly entwines us directly with one another.

The suggested model to pair human individuality and relationality is the theological rubric "union with Christ," particularly as expressed ecclesiologically in communion.

### Limit-Pair Three (LP3): Agency and Automation

The third limit-pair is labelled "Agency and Automation." On the one hand, IA must not impinge upon a human agency. Humans must maintain the ability for autonomy of mind and will. But yet core to Christian anthropology is the view that humanity is sinful, and human agency in particular is corrupted. On the other hand, it is posited that human automation - here, efficiency in information technology - is an implicit and warranted extrapolation of humans as technological beings (per LP1). But yet today, Artificial Intelligence, that forms the peak of cognitive automation, poses a great existential threat.

In pairing these two limits together, this project (provocatively) concludes that it is those in Christ - whose minds and wills are positionally and continuously in the only person to be free from corruption - that are ontologically uniquely suited to extend IA; pairing agency and automation towards a "Christocentric cognitive technology."

Crucially, these limit-pairs are offered as a framework for theological discourse -what it means to be human as IA accelerates. By design, they sketch and uncomfortably stretch the "limits" for technological engagement on the path ahead, whilst remaining explicitly anchored-and dependent-on Christ.

## Model Design

### Immanence and Transcendence Pairs

This Christian Anthropology is structured around two dual (though not dualistic) threads of Christian theology - "immanence" and "transcendence." These limits are not intended as two opposing forces to be balanced against each other, but rather both are simultaneously affirmed and are mutually reinforcing.

Indeed, this immanence-transcendence design finds support in and out of theology. So, Ronald Cole-Turner sees this duality running through the very heart of the Christian theology of God. Sensing the tension in the varying perspectives of twelve Christian contributors to *Transhumanism and Transcendence*, he notes that the tension:

> is something that Christian theology tends to locate in the very nature of God... God expresses both frameworks, as the Creator who redeems and transforms... When Christian theologians find themselves drawn to both... it is because they see this polarity as foundational not just to their view of humanity but also of the God in whose image we are created.[^12]

Similarly, Brent Waters' theological of the Posthuman notes: 'On the one hand there is an impetus to preserve... on the other hand there is a need to be prospective.'[^13] While Erik Parens' survey of the secular human enhancement debate, sees it as revolving around two frameworks: a framework of "gratitude" that 'emphasises our obligation to remember that life is a gift and that we need to learn to let things be,' and a framework of "creativity" that 'emphasizes our obligation to transform that gift and to exhibit our creativity.'[^14]

Continuing in this tradition, but seeing the tension as intrinsic to Christian theology (not just secular ethics), this project adopts overtly religious language of "immanence" and "transcendence."[^15] Where, on the one hand, "immanence" affirms 'God's presence and activity within nature, human nature, and history... He is the God of nature, of natural law. God is present throughout the natural universe (Jer.23:24) and especially so with living creatures (Acts.17:27-28), creating and sustaining their life (Gen.2:7; Job.27:3, 33:4, 34:14-15; Ps.104:29-30);'[^16] and, on the other hand, "transcendence" affirms a God that is superior to and beyond nature and humanity. God's transcendence often describes his goodness, holiness and purity, but here the focus encompasses his power and knowledge - he whose thoughts are higher than our thoughts, and ways higher than our ways (Isa.55:8-9).

Indeed, the pairing of this immanence-transcendence is central to the Christian tradition - it is Jesus himself who anthropologically manifests and enacts this paradox in history: speaking at the turn of the century, in the city of Jerusalem, Jesus can still say - 'I am not of this world!'

And so, all three Limit-Pairs culminate in the immanent-and-transcendent Christ. Both objectively in his person and work - he forms the exemplar and paradigm through which anthropology (including cyber-anthropology) can be seen and understood; and, subjectively through participation in union with Christ - Christianity finds the language and experience of "hybridity" as Christ is "in us" and "with us."

### Limits

The immanence and transcendence threads are presented as "limits" (hence "limit-pair"). This limits model for "anthropology of enhancement" follows from Deborah Creamer's "anthropology of disability" which highlights how all people are limited, only to varying degrees: humans being are defined by limitation - be they able-bodied, disabled or, extending the implications for our current study, super-abled.

> In the limits model, the fact that all people are limited to varying degrees is highlighted... We recognize that it is not only those who are labelled "disabled" that experience limits; limits are something inherent in the experience of humanity... understood to be part of creation... The importance of this model is its demand that limits, as well as the diversity of ability, must be seen as integral elements of our understandings of self and other.[^17]

Describing immanence and transcendence as "limits," brings counter-cultural perspective that helps to navigate often unspoken presumption that permeates Transhumanist ethos - that "limited" always equals "less," and "unlimited" always equals "better." But theologically, as Creamer notes, 'limits ought not to be tied uncritically to interpretations of value and worth.'[^18] For even God took limits willingly by taking on personhood (and death) through Jesus. Indeed, his resurrected body, though perfect, maintained the scars of his death.[^19]

More explicitly, limits help our navigation in two interrelated ways. Firstly, limits function as boundaries: the immanence limits for IA demonstrates that which is human that must not be diminished, while transcendence limits for IA act to curb any hubris of obtaining spiritual transcendence through humanity alone - true transcendence can only come from God. Secondly, limits function as trajectories: vectors and goals that direct humans towards becoming fully human. Cognitively augmented humans are most human within these boundaries and trajectories.

## A Note on Theological Method

Insofar as possible the proposed anthropological model will be informed by direct biblical theology and traditional Christian doctrines in its discourse (extrapolating from "biblical judgements" towards the theological "concepts" applicable for IA).[^20] This reliance on an ultimately biblical epistemological foundation helps avoid the fragility of an overly-speculative theology being used to discuss an already speculative technology. Erring in this direction has a two-fold result: (1) this project takes a position in line with creedal Orthodox Christianity, but (2) this project is limited in its capacities towards resolving any pre-existing anthropological debates of "mind" and "body," debates that might at first seem central to a study of IA.[^21]

Nevertheless, it is hoped that such an emphasis begins to fill a gap in the currently speculative efforts 'to discern a theological anthropology of human-technology integration.'[^22] Indeed, from the research conducted, a systematic approach from biblical theology seems underrepresented in theological studies of Transhumanism, resulting in a lopsided anthropology with a tendency towards substantive views of the human over relational and functional ones.[^23]

## Limit-Pair One: Embodiment and Transformation

Limit Pair One (LP1) argues that embodiment is anthropologically necessary to the restorative and teleological metanarratives of Scripture, but (provocatively) biologically constrained embodiment is not a clear-cut boundary. Indeed, if humans, as co-creators, are inherently technological beings and technology is inherently transformative, then humans are inherently self-transformative. Though the concept "technology" is a recent observation, with hindsight technological transformation and mediation is present throughout Scripture. However, for IA in particular, knowledge by itself does not free but enslave. Antithetical to Transhumanist hope in cognitive enhancement for humans to become god-like, the Christian model is of an all-knowing God who became human like us.

### Immanence Limit: Humans as Embodied Beings

#### Connectome: The Brain is the Mind

Neuroscience, physics, and bioinformatics expert Sebastian Seung notes the progression of Enlightenment thought in the history of science:

> Over the centuries, science has shaken our belief in the soul. First we were told [by materialism], "You are a bunch of atoms"... Then biology and neuroscience told us, "You are a machine"... But computers have forced us to re-examine the doctrines of materialism and mechanism. "You are a bunch of information". you are neither machine nor matter. Those are just means of storing what you really are—information.[^24]

Recently however, Computational Neuroscience has shown such mechanistic reductionism is unwarranted; Seung's research demonstrates that a straightforward disconnect between brain and mind is overly simplistic. The structure and function of the brain are intricately linked - 'the foundation for human cognition lies in the pattern of dynamic interactions shaped by the connectome,'[^25] where the term "connectome" describes the map of the neural connections of the brain and nervous system across multiple scales; the connectome maps the mind as the genome maps the body. In this newer conception, the network of the brain and nervous system in essence *are* the mind.[^26]

Importantly for this theological project, this newer conception of entwined mind-body materialism finds compatibility and indeed affinity with Christian theology. For Christianity, distinct from dualist or gnostic philosophies, has historically upheld and defended the goodness of created matter, and the necessity of embodiment in being human.

#### Embodiment in Biblical Metanarratives: Teleological and Restorative

Here then we must bring to bear two Christian meta-narratives upon our anthropological model. For ultimately in Christian theology, the nature and purposes of humanity are inextricably tied to the nature and purposes of God as revealed in his Word.

The first meta-narrative is teleological; a growth movement from Genesis to Revelation - from creation in the garden of Eden to new creation and city of Zion, God destines people and nature for something greater. The second meta-narrative is restorative; a redemptive movement of creation-fall-salvation of both persons and nature - created good but corrupted by sin, God restores what humans broke. And both of these movements find their apex in the person and work of Christ - the living word and the embodiment of God in a human. In Christ, Christian anthropology finds form and meaning.

So, the doctrine of *imago dei* - whether defined substantively, relationally or functionally - affirms the universality of all humanity as created "good" in the image of God with intrinsic worth (Gen. 1:26-27).[^27] The doctrine of original sin, conversely testifies to the universality of sin as a result of the fall - through Adam and Eve's disobedience, all humans are sinners, relationship with God and each other are fractured, and creation itself is corrupted (Gen. 3).

Yet Christology affirms the Creator himself incarnate as a human being into his creation (John 1:1-18); a 'last Adam' whose obedience in life and death takes the penalty of all humanity's sin. His bodily resurrection, as the 'first-born of the new creation,' both restores and transforms humanity into a new humanity (2 Cor. 5:17) - imperishable and spiritual (1 Cor. 15:42-50); through him the corrupted creation itself is reconciled (Col 1:19-20). Ecclesiologically, the Church of believers represent present citizens of his future new creation; their fellowship is demonstrative of a spiritual unity as diverse members of a united 'body of Christ,' where relationships with God and one another are restored.

A Christian anthropology of IA must be built upon these two Christ-centric meta-narratives. Reflecting the role of humans in the overarching biblical gospel story brings support from the entirety of Scripture and avoids the misapplication or overextension of sub-ordinate doctrines.[^28]

Embodiment is fundamental to these two major meta-narratives of Scripture. Humans are created, and created as embodied beings, and an expectation of embodiment extends into new creation. Paralleling the conception of the "Connectome," human minds are never detached from material embodiment.

The Genesis creation account sees Adam being formed from the 'dust of the ground' (Gen.2:7). In the New Testament, against gnostic conceptions, the body is affirmed (1Cor.6:12-20). This is epitomised by Jesus - the eternal God incarnate 'in the flesh.' (Jn.1:14; 1Jn.1:1, 4:2-3), who undergoes a physical bodily resurrection (Lk.24:38-39; Jn.20:27). So too, in Christian eschatology, the final destination for humans is not an escape from bodily existence into some ethereal heavenly realm of souls; rather, material creation is re-affirmed and renewed. Following Jesus, Christians likewise expect a new body (Rom.8:11, 22-23) in new creation.

Ironically, the tongue-in-cheek caricature of Transhumanism as a "rapture of the nerds" bears some affinity with Christian expectations for new creation that affirms continuity of the body and identity. Indeed, akin to Transhumanism's hopes, Christian theology sees both Jesus' resurrected body and our own eschatological bodies as remaining embodied yet somehow also fully glorious; the same, yet transformed (Phil.3:20-21).

This final observation opens up questions about what "embodiment" means for IA - does embodiment necessarily refer to an organic brain of flesh and blood? It is argued that though embodiment is intrinsic and necessary to remain true to the Christian view of a human, the medium for this embodiment can - and it will be argued, already does - exceed the organic brain. Indeed, non-biological embodiment has biblical precedent, finding "compatibility" with Christian theology.

#### Compatible Theological Precedent: Eschatological Bodies

The heart of the "compatibility" argument lies in the ambiguity and mystery regarding the nature of our recreated bodies; those resurrected in new creation. Though embodiment is maintained in new creation, these new bodies are different and superior to our current ones, as epitomised by Christ.

1 Corinthians 15:42-50 states that these resurrected bodies will not be perishable but imperishable; not a natural body, but a spiritual body. Indeed verses 44-48 highlight a progression from natural to spiritual - 'The spiritual did not come first, but the natural, and after that the spiritual' (v45). The former, from the first Adam, of the dust of the earth; the latter, from the last Adam (Jesus), of heaven. Incisively for this project, Paul's conclusion from this is that 'flesh and blood cannot inherit the Kingdom of God.'

At a minimum, 1 Corinthians 15:42-50 notes some limitation with existing flesh and blood bodies that are incapable of coping with the glory of God, and therefore affirms embodiment of a different kind, beyond death and decay. To be clear, this project is not suggesting that future spiritual bodies are akin to machine augmented bodies. But, nevertheless, we find in this passage preliminary precedent for compatibility; human embodied can exist beyond the organic body in its current form.

And, the first of these imperishable bodies is that of Jesus himself (Phil.3:20-21). So, while the anthropocentric example of 1 Corinthians 15:42-50 is future oriented and obscured, we are afforded a historical glimpse of how this might look in the resurrected body of Jesus whose living "flesh and bone" body (Lk.24:39) still somehow maintained the mortal wounds of his death (Jn.20:27). As Michael Bird notes, 'He did not revert back to the heavenly and incorporeal form of the preexistent Son of God after his ascension,' he maintains an enduring human character even amidst divine glory (Acts.7:56; Rev.1:13).[^29] Instead, Jesus' glory is fully realised in his risen form - appearing inside locked rooms immediately post resurrection (Jn.20:19) and manifest in light post ascension (Acts.9:1-7). An incarnate human, Christ Jesus, can somehow mediate and rule from the right hand of the Father (1Tim.2:5).

Turning from bodily transformation to more specifically consider cognitive extension, an examination of Jesus' "knowledge" during his earthly ministry provides a mysterious precedent where "mind" exceeds biology:

> He knew the thoughts of his friends (Lk.9:47) and his enemies (Lk.6:8). He could read the character of Nathanael (Jn.1:47-48). He "did not need any testimony about mankind, for he knew what was in each person" (Jn.2:25). He knew that the Samaritan woman had had five husbands... (Jn.4:18). He knew that Lazarus was already dead (Jn.11:14). He knew that Judas would betray him (Matt.26:25) and that Peter would deny him (Matt.26:34). Indeed, Jesus knew all that was to happen to him (Jn.18:4).[^30]

Again, I am not suggesting Jesus' divine knowledge is akin to machine knowledge through IA, but merely that a precedent exists, specifically in Christ, where we see that knowledge can lie beyond fleshy nature.[^31] So, not only does general bodily augmentation find precedent, but also cognitive extension is not incompatible. Moreover, the extensions beyond biological boundaries, in both the anthropocentric and Christocentric examples discussed, are shown in a biblically positive light. The hopes for bodies beyond flesh and blood, and for minds beyond our limited understanding are compatible with Christian theology and not dismissed outright.

So, though (obviously) the bible nowhere affirms the employment of computational technology to enhance intelligence, Scripture nevertheless acknowledges some sense of biological limitation, and affirms human hopefulness in overcoming them. Clearly, the two examples above - our bodies in new creation, and Jesus' extended knowledge - are of a different order from that of the cybernetic technologies typically associated with IA.[^32] As Wesley Smith warns, these examples are talking of spiritual natures (indeed, in the Hypostatic Union it is divine nature!), and conflating spiritual transformation with physical/technological transformation risks replacing God's plans for eschatological recreation with our desires for progress through human effort.[^33] Nevertheless, the intention here is merely to demonstrate that having the mind's existence and embodiment beyond flesh and blood bodies is not without precedent in the Christian tradition.[^34]

In order to give shape to this embodiment conclusion, it is helpful to contrast it against Jeanine Thweatt-Bates' own Christian Posthuman anthropology that uses embodiment to draw a sharp distinction between her conception of "cyborg" and "uploaded consciousness." She argues that the "cyborg," as an extension of the human with the non-human (i.e. embodied), is theologically viable, whereas "uploaded consciousness," that seeks to shed the 'problematic biological body for a virtual existence or a more durable artificial body,' is theologically untenable.[^35] This project shares Thweatt-Bates' concern for embodiment, but not her conclusion. In particular, her rejection of "uploaded consciousness" seems based on the assumption that embodiment must be biological.

As argued, this seems presumptive.[^36] Moreover, presuming that embodiment must be biological risks framing her own concept of "cyborg" as a sort of partial embodiment. This seems theologically contentious against the biblical affirmation of humans as unitary (or at least constitutionally unified) beings, pressingly so if we conceive of humans as, in a sense, already cyborgs. By contrast, this project's view of embodiment maintains unitary nature even amidst any pre-existing non-biological extensions.[^37]

The question that remains, however, is whether physical externalities beyond our current bodies and minds are explicitly reserved for the spiritual realm, or can humans "self-extend" their bodies and minds? Is body-mind transformation necessarily equivalent to human hubris? It is also a question of timing and kind. Is transformation beyond biological intelligence solely the domain of a future and final spiritual recreation? Or is there warrant for human transformation through technology today? Such questions move us towards the "transcendence" half of LP1.

### Transcendence Limit: Humans as Transformed by Technology

Having established compatibility of non-biological embodiment in biblical precedent; the transcendence limit looks to see if there is warrant for the transformation of human beings through technological augmentation (specifically information technology) beyond its created form.

#### Warrant for Technological Transformation

For transformation warrant, we first observe human progress and growth as general themes in early theology. Then, fast-forwarding to modern times we use the technological taxonomy of Kline, filtered theologically through Dyer, to demonstrate the pervasive and inherent transforming and mediating effect of technology - upon humanity and society.

With hindsight, the abstracted conception of "technology" can be seen as intrinsic even in the creation account of Genesis 2-3 such that general theological themes of progress and growth implicitly include a technological element. As such it is posited that, if (1) technology is inherently transformative upon humans, and (2) humans are created as technological, then (3) humans are created as technologically self-transformative. For an anthropology of IA, this defines humans as beings that already augment their intelligence; humans, as fundamentally self-transforming, are already "cognitive cyborgs."

#### Christian anthropology of transformation...

Though the redemptive metanarrative - the sinful nature of humans - complicates matters and (rightly) tempers expectations, Christianity's teleological metanarrative, described earlier, provides broad biblical support for human growth and transformation. And this teleological "growth" emphasis finds theological roots as early as the Patristic Fathers.

So Origen (c.185-c.254), despite stating 'everyone who enters the world may be said to be affected by a kind of contamination [of sin],'[^38] nevertheless sees humanity as created in the "image" of God but intended to reach the "likeness" of God towards ultimate fulfilment for the final consummation.[^39] Tertullian (c.160-c.225) similarly juxtaposes "image" and "likeness" to contrast humanity's created versus eternal state.

> Thus humanity is restored to God into "his likeness," for he had originally been "in his image." The state of being "in the image of God" relates to his form; "in his likeness" refers to his eternity.[^40]

Greggory of Nyssa (c.330-c.395) sees our longing as a desire for a return to 'the good' of the paradise we lost in Eden, a theme later picked up as spiritual renewal by John Scotus (c.810-c.877) and more recently as relational restoration by Emil Brunner (1889-1966).[^41] And, perhaps most famously, Irenaeus (c.130-c.200), laid the most explicit and earliest groundwork promoting teleological progress - perfection had to take place through a process of moral and spiritual growth; humanity was not created perfect, but was created for perfection.[^42]

Though not without criticism,[^43] it is clear that the concept of human growth and transformation is ingrained in Christian theology from the outset, even amidst consensus that perfection ultimately requires spiritual restoration through God's intervention.

#### Technology as inherently transformative...

Though transformation has been intrinsic to Christian anthropology from its beginning, these early conceptions mentioned above tend to focus, either explicitly or implicitly, on moral transformation (if stretched, arguably extending to relational, communal or cultural growth). Fast forwarding from the Patristic Fathers to modern times in the early 21st century, however, there are pressing questions regarding the place (if any) of technology within humanity's teleological metanarrative. Generally, such questions take an ethical dimension - i.e. whether technology should or should not be used. However, this paper argues that technology (particularly cognitive technology) should be considered, first and foremost, theologically, as intrinsic to anthropology - i.e. who we are.

Bringing "technology" within biblical theology is a frustrating exercise in extrapolation. After all, the very idea of "technology" is a modern one, having crystalized at the turn of the 20th century. Prior to this, the term "technology" was uncommon, limited to describing the study of manufacturing/crafting techniques. But towards the end of the Second Industrial Revolution (c.1867-c.1914) the term "technology" grew in scope; beyond describing the study of production, towards also encompassing the production techniques themselves and also the products they produced. Thus "technology" became a general and abstracted, yet visible, concept; describing all tools, and the tools and techniques that make those tools. A common definition still used by sociologists comes from American sociologist Read Bain in 1937:[^44]

> Technology includes all tools, machines, utensils, weapons, instruments, clothing, communicating and transporting devices and the skills by which we produce and use them.

Today, less than a century later, with sustained and accelerating advancement in "technology," what was a conceptual abstraction is now increasingly apparent - profoundly "real" and unavoidably manifest in modern society. It is now obvious to talk about and study technology and its effects in a way that would have sounded absurdly unnecessary and abstract to our forebears. Moreover, from this modern vantage point we can now more readily perceive, and personally experience, how technology might be described as inherently transformative - to society, environment and culture (relationship, creation, values) and, by implication, unavoidably transformative upon the human being. Showing remarkable foresight, the sentences immediately preceding and following Bain's definition of "technology" predict this pervasive influence of technology (emphasis mine):[^45]

> **Broadly conceived, technology is the most important single factor in producing, integrating and destroying cultural phenomena...**
> Social institutions and their so-called non-material concomitants such as values, morals, manners, wishes, hopes, fears and attitudes **are directly and indirectly dependent upon technology and are mediated by it.**

Philosopher Stephen J. Kline further refines the influence and mediating effect of technology by describing four ways the term "technology" can be used. Using John Dyer's (simplified) gloss of Kline's categories: technology as (1) hardware, (2) manufacturing, (3) methodology and (4) social use.[^46] The pervasiveness and interplay of technological influence can be more clearly seen through this four-fold schema. The examples cited will begin to connect these categories towards the domain of current cognitive technology.

*   **Hardware or Artefacts ("hardware")** - the most basic level of technology that describes non-natural objects of all kinds, manufactured by humans - e.g. written language, books (e.g. a printed Bible), eBooks and computer algorithms. Dyer, citing Kline, clarifies the distinction between "natural" and "unnatural" as, respectively, what God has made, and what we make out of God's creation.[^47]
*   **Sociotechnical systems of manufacture ("manufacturing")** - all elements of a system needed to manufacture a particular piece of hardware including people, machinery, resources, processes, and legal/economic/political and physical environment. Though manufacturing technology no doubt existed prior to the Industrial Revolution - e.g. ancient chisel upon stone tablets - at this second layer, we begin to see today's manufacturing technology can bring abstracted concepts into sharp relief - e.g. from the printing press to the sprawling "cities" of electronic tablet manufacturers.[^48]
*   **Knowledge, Technique, Know-How, or Methodology ("methodology")** - the information, skills, processes and procedures for accomplishing tasks. Distinct from Technology as Manufacturing, this layer employs human knowledge to understand the routines, methods and skills of technology use - i.e. how does it all work together well? (For example, the information and processes behind carpentry, fishing and tent-making.) This third layer demonstrates how "manufacturing" technology has a mediating influence upon "methodology" technology - the tools you have at your disposal, though helpful, also restrict and guide the way you create. So, evolutionary "machine learning" algorithms, though powerful, are subject to the amount and type of data available leading to encoded heuristic bias.
*   **A Sociotechnical System of Use Technology as Use ("Social Use")** - Kline's final layer that describes the combining of the other three layers to accomplish tasks that humans cannot otherwise perform - 'to extend human capacities.'[^49] In a sense this last category most directly describes the transformative nature of technology upon humans - both intentionally and unintentionally. So, transportation systems, electronic banking, and the operating systems on our mobile phones all transform and extend human and societal capacities, yet they also (like technology of methodology) mediate and restrict those same areas of our life that they extend.

So, technology usage implies transformation. And the interplay between these four types of usage amplifies technology's transformative power. "Hardware" mediates its human usage and "manufacturing" mediates the type of hardware produced (paper technology influences the type/length of epistles as the hardware/software user interface mediates how I write this paper); and the mediating effects of "hardware" and "manufacturing" are reinforced by "methodology" and systematised in "social use". Taken together, we might summarise Kline's definition of technology as "applying methodology to manufacture hardware for human use," while Dyer reframes this within an explicitly Christian framework - "the human activity of using tools to transform God's creation for practical purposes."

Turning to IA technology, computers and recent information/communication technologies (ICT) simultaneously interact across all four layers. IA technology is hardware (or software), controlling systems of manufacture, storing repositories of knowledge for methodology, and connecting society's communications and networks. More than that, in IA technology acts as an extension of "self" the transformative effect is profoundly acute. As Cole-Turner notes:

> ...when technology is now turned inward upon ourselves, on our bodies and brains, we are changed more directly, more intentionally, and in the end more profoundly than ever before.[^50]

Yet, paradoxically, the sharpness of insight from current and future technology, helps us better observe technological use in our past. The four-fold schema finds that technology is equally applicable to "high-tech" society and ancient society. The transformative nature of technology is intrinsic; it is unavoidable and it always has been. So, both Kline and Dyer note three profound consequences of this definition of technology: (1) technology intrinsically extends human capacities, (2) technology is not new, but extends back to human origins (variously defined), (3) technology is uniquely human.

So, Kline, seeing human uniqueness in biological evolution, observes:[^51]

> The pattern of [technology] in order to extend our human capacities is not a product of the high-tech age. On the contrary, the pattern was first adopted by our evolutionary ancestors... Other animals use sociotechnical systems (beavers, ants, bees and prairie dogs to mention only a few). However, we humans are the only species that purposefully makes innovations in our sociotechnical systems in order to (hopefully) improve their functioning. This characteristic of purposeful innovation in sociotechnical systems, distinguishes humans from other animals as least as clearly as any single characteristic.

While Dyer, draws similar conclusions from doctrines of creation, culture mandate, and *imago dei* - capturing this in Christian anthropological terms:[^52]

> [technology] is a special subset of the command to create and cultivate the garden, and it began with Adam's creation of language.

Moving forward with Dyer, we turn to the creation narrative to support his definition of technology.

#### ...in hindsight, an anthropology of technological transformation

Dyer helpfully cites three "technological" snippets from Genesis 2 and 3 from which theological insight can be gleaned. Note that the arguments presented in this paper are intended to be compatible regardless of a literal or a mythic/poetic interpretation of Genesis.

Firstly, human transformation of nature is natural. Adam was created to cultivate the Garden of Eden (Gen.2:15, Cf. Gen.1:28), therefore nature is dynamic at human hands. The garden was to be worked on and so, presumably, changed from its original created state, and important this command to cultivate the garden occurs pre-fall-i.e. cultivation exceeds any "maintenance" requirement from sin, death and decay. This example presents a hurdle for those who would equate what is "natural", with creation in its static, original form. Instead, what is "natural," even prior to the entry of sin, presupposes transformation at the hand of humanity. Presuming cultivation could move beyond Adam using his "bare hands," technology is therefore unavoidable and implicit in the cultural mandate of Genesis 1:28 to fill and subdue creation, of which humans are at once part of (created alongside cosmos, plants and animals) yet ontologically distinct from (by God's creational decree).

Secondly, citing language as the "the first technology," Dyer sees evidence of Adam's vice-creation in using language to name the animals - God brought living creatures to Adam to 'see what he would name them; and whatever the man called each living creature, that was its name' (Gen.2:19-20). Here we have an explicit example of humans using and enhancing the tool of language - actively shaping (a proto information) technology alongside God.[^53] So, the Christian creation narrative sees technology as creational from the beginning as God speaks to create, and Adam invents new words in an act subordinate of vice-creation; at once showing the similarity and the difference between the powerful immediacy of God's speech-act, and the limited human form through spoken language - of the same kind, but a different order.[^54] Moreover, as Jardine notes, this vice-creational view of technology in Genesis stands distinct from pagan myth where tools/technology are given by the gods or stolen from gods. So, the Christian metanarrative is uniquely suited to inform modern technological transformational concerns.[^55]

Thirdly, and now immediately post-fall, we see that there still remains room for goodness of technology in what Dyer calls "the first technology upgrade." God recreates the fig-leaf garments made by Adam and Eve to hide their shame, transforming them into "state-of-the-art" garments of skin (Gen.3:21).[^56] So, despite sin, and here even in a human invention that was a direct result of sin, God demonstrates that technology itself can still be used for good. Indeed, his act of grace is mediated through a technology. Indeed, depictions of "good" technology use continues, often unnoticed, through Scripture - examples include: Noah's Ark, written records of the law, tabernacle blueprints, the temple of Jerusalem, the ships and roads used for the outreach of the early Church; Jesus was a carpenter, his apostles were fishermen, Paul made tents.[^57]

Of course, the bible also presents negative examples of technology use (the Tower of Babel being the classic example). But for our purposes at this juncture, it can be stated that there is biblical warrant for seeing technology, and technological growth, as part of God's plans and purposes for humans.

As a brief aside as we close, it is important to explicitly reiterate the difference in this project's methodology from the methodology from dynamic "evolutionary technonature" employed by many other Posthuman theologies.[^58] Though my conclusion bears similarities, its formulation is attempting to actively avoid any post-modernist tendencies towards a plastic *imago dei* that reinvents itself to suit the times (inherently dynamic as evolution is dynamic). Modern observation of technology, it is argued, gives the benefit of hindsight, but not freedom for radical reinterpretation. The intention is avoid epistemologically building upon evolutionary theology (in effect sacralising evolution / experience). Observing modern technology, this project is not saying that we "therefore" need to redefine humans; nor is it saying that the embrace of technology is a necessary trajectory of evolution (whether natural or divinely guided). Instead, with hindsight, technology is intrinsic to the biblical creation account (and visibility across the biblical narrative) and therefore technology is intrinsic to what it means to be human.

#### ...with foresight, already cognitive cyborgs

Drawing the threads together, (1) from a biblical perspective we are created technologically transformational (fitting within general growth theologies of the Patristics); and, (2) from a modern vantage this "technology" can be seen to have a mediating and transformative effect, even upon humans. This then leads to the conclusion that (3) humans are ontologically self-transformative. This is the "transformation limit" of LP1.[^59] Whilst the impact, speed, and directness of technology's transformative effect have changed through history - and lie on a spectrum; not all technology is created equal - humans and technology have never been wholly separate.

Moreover, in applying this technological self-transformation limit specifically to the cognitive technologies of IA, we similarly conclude: information technology already transforms us.[^60] As part of the complex network of social media, mobile technologies and the internet we are already human-technology symbionts; in a sense, already cognitive cyborgs.[^61] And the definition even encompasses that most rudimentary of information technologies - written language. Ted Chiang's words capture eloquently what many have experienced with increased language fluency:

> We don't normally think of it as such, but writing is a technology, which means that a literate person is someone whose thought processes are technologically mediated. We became cognitive cyborgs as soon as we became fluent readers, and the consequences of that were profound.[^62]

The historical lineage of IA can be traced from written language to machine-learning, from the abacus to the internet; all act as extensions to amplify human information processing capacities. Even without any physical machine-brain interface, this "soft" IA has been inescapably transformative, and tracing this continuum from our "soft-augmented" anthropological origins helps us frame how we engage with the emerging, obvious, and urgent forms of "hard" augmentation today.

As modern information technologies accelerate, creating unpredictable and increasingly unavoidable impact,[^63] recalibrating our anthropological expectations offers an opportunity to reflect and intentionally guide technology to be formative, not just intrinsically transformative.

### Pairing the Limits: Embodied Transformers

Having established embodiment and transformation limits for IA, this project now briefly suggests Christian implications for pairing the limits. Admittedly, this begins moving from theology and encroaches upon ethics, yet ethical questions are still framed by anthropological values. In particular, LP1 relocates the hopes for transformation via Intelligence Amplification, specifically questioning whether reason automatically make us more human.

So, for example, when Brent Waters contends that the Posthuman is decidedly anti-human he is primarily arguing against the "motivations and hope" that broadly permeates much of the Transhumanism movement that sees all natural limitations as something to be overcome through radical technological transformation. For the "cognitive augmentation" strand of Transhumanism in particular, this becomes an extension of enlightenment ideals - a hope of transcendence through human reason and knowledge.[^64]

This project shares Waters' concerns regarding Transhumanism's humanistic motivation/hope - particularly IA's hope in human reason and knowledge. For, as Isaiah 55:8-9 notes, God's thoughts are higher than our own as the heavens are higher than the earth (Cf. 1Cor.1:25). Indeed, it is why this anthropological model frames "transformation" as a limit, as limits define a trajectory - a vector and goal - that can direct IA transformation within biblical normative boundaries; transformation may be infinite, but it is not unlimited. As Waters rightly notes:

> Humans are creatures that God has elected to oversee the providential unfolding of God's creation. This does not imply that God has authorized humans to master and shape creation in whatever form they might desire... they have been chosen by God to be the agents that align creation to its created order.[^65]

#### Knowledge and Grace

Indeed, surveying the place of reason and knowledge within Scripture should give one pause. Returning to the creation account, the first sin of Adam and Eve is their eating the fruit from the "tree of the knowledge of good and evil" (Gen.3:1-8). The fruit was 'desirable for gaining wisdom,' and when they ate it 'the eyes of both of them were open' (vv.6-7). On the one hand demonstrating the truth in Satan's words 'your eyes will be opened, and you will be like God, knowing good and evil,' (v.1) on the other hand his lies. For though the fruit of knowledge seemed desirable, in and of itself its consumption brought immediate shame and separation - hiding from their God (v.7-8). Their subsequent banishment from Eden is also instructive (vv.22-23a):

> And the LORD God said, "The man has now become like one of us, knowing good and evil. He must not be allowed to reach out his hand and take also from the tree of life and eat, and live forever." So the LORD God banished him from the Garden of Eden

Biblical theologians have interpreted this banishment as a mercy and grace.[^66] Of the two named trees in the garden, the commandment against eating only applied to the Tree of Knowledge of Good and Evil; there was freedom to eat from all other trees, presumably including the Tree of Life (Gen.2:16-17). Yet after eating the fruit of knowledge, banishment occurs lest they now eat from the tree of life. Life, even eternal life, with knowledge alone is not glorious but ignominious. Moral autonomy apart from God prevents life and introduces death.[^67]

This is not to say that the bible treats knowledge as intrinsically evil; specifically, it is knowledge that is disconnected from God. By contrast, knowledge connected with God is defined as wisdom (e.g. Prov.1:7) and its affirmation throughout the Wisdom Literature of Scripture is considerable - in song (Psalms), in practical guidance (Proverbs) and contrasted through historical narrative (e.g. Job, 1 and 2 Kings). Against the Transhumanist metanarrative of reason as pinnacle of evolution, the Christian metanarrative positions knowledge-without-God as intrinsic to the fall of humanity.[^68]

#### Culminating in Christ

And so, the biblical narrative could be framed as a story of continued mercy and compassion from God amidst humanities misdirected knowledge. Indeed, Scriptures cite mercy, humility and submission as intrinsic to wisdom (Jm.3:13, 17). True transcendence is not achieved by "doubling down" to amplify intelligence, but is ultimately realised in Christ and reliant upon his work of grace to restore true knowledge.

Sebastian Seung, who we used to introduce LP1, contrasts Transhumanism ideals against the biblical conception of humanity:[^69]

> The Bible said that God made man in his own image. The German philosopher Ludwig Feuerbach said that man made God in his own image. The Transhumanists say that humanity itself will make itself into a God.

While this limit-pair suggests that Transhumanist ideas/worldviews are actually not so dissimilar in substance, Seung's quote reveals a common sentiment within Transhumanism that is incompatible with Christianity. This incompatibility is not in the intrinsic desire to become god-like - Christianity similarly holds eschatological desire to "become like God;" nor is it in the technological nature of that self-transformation - LP1 argues that technological self-transformation is ontological to humans; rather, the incompatibility lies in the instrumental self-reliance of "making oneself" into a god (i.e. human hubris). By contrast, Christian transformation, even technological self-transformation, is reliant upon Christ, where the meek shall inherit the earth (Matt.5:1-12).[^70]

So in Christ we encounter a paradox. Where Transhumanist's seek to increase human cognitive capacity towards "god-like" function and capability, Christianity instead finds a God who intentionally limits his capacities; an omniscient and omnipotent God who is incarnate as a human being, Jesus Christ - the word of God (true knowledge itself) become flesh. A God who was born into humanity; a God who died for humanity. Contrary to common Transhumanist ideals of strength today, and the Hellenistic ideals of strength of his day, Jesus preached 'blessed are the poor in spirit, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven... blessed are the meek, for they will inherit the earth' (Matt.5:3-12) and 'whoever takes the lowly position of this child is the greatest in the kingdom of heaven' (Matt.18:1-7).

Hence, even as the anthropology of LP1 affirms technological self-transformation, it does so within Christ-directed limits; working negatively against the precipitous tendency toward hubris, and positively towards the future glory of new heaven and new earth.

## Limit-Pair Two: Individual and Relational

Where LP1 concluded that, within a Christian anthropology, technological self-transformation is maximised when Christ-directed, LP2 argues that this "directedness" does not come from some external value ethic, but through existentially transformative communion with Christ. That is, human individuals are restored and find full expression in relational "union with Christ."

Formally then, LP2 proposes an anthropology of IA that promotes human "individuality" and "relationality" which together culminate in Christ, particularly as expressed in ecclesial communion with Christ.

Where LP1 laid broad support for the transformative nature of IA, LP2 and LP3 consider how IA maps to Christian Anthropology more specifically. Key questions of LP2 include whether cognitive machine augmentations are considered part of an individual's identity; and how such machine augmentation should be viewed when the machine elements might be shared across many individuals; and does (hyper) interconnectedness improve or erode human capacity for relationship?

### Immanence Limit: Humans as Individual Persons

John Habgood notes two meanings of the word "individual."[^71] In the first sense "individual" means "indivisible" or "uncuttable" - 'that which cannot be divided into parts without destroying its essential nature;' so humans are the indivisible smallest unit of humanity; splitting a human means they are no longer human. In the second sense "individual" means "particular" or "distinctive" - 'each thing is what it is and not another thing;' so, for humans, each person is distinct and separate from other persons. Both senses of the word will be used to define the immanence limit of LP2: humans are, and must always remain, individuals.

The first "indivisible" or "uncuttable" meaning of the "individual" limit is examined through theologies of human constitution, framing IA as constitutionally feasible as an "extension of self" irrespective of philosophical debates regarding monism and dichotomism (and trichotomism).

However, in "machine augmentation" this first sense of "individual" is tightly coupled with its second sense. If an individual's constitution can be said to extend beyond their own biological body onto shared machine hardware/software then the "particular" or "distinctive" boundaries of the "individual" become blurry; if one computer is used to augment two humans, does human-machine hybridity become human-human hybridity? An "anthropology of IA" can no longer treat both definitions of "individuality" as mutually exclusive.

So, to study the individual in its second particular/distinctive sense we again return to the twin biblical metanarratives presented in LP1, finding that human particularity is affirmed both teleologically in creation and new creation, and restoratively in sin and redemption. Moreover, both metanarratives culminate in a type of existential hybridity - "Union with Christ." This theological rubric forms an analogical precedent for person-person hybridity, maintaining human individuality even while existentially connected in, and sharing identity with, another.[^72]

#### Body, Soul and Spirit... and Mind

Locating an argument for IA within traditional models of constitution is messy.

Firstly, the neurobiological scientific view would situation IA as a cognitive extension of the mind, where the mind is explicitly connected (one and the same) with the biological body - i.e. the mind, if anything, is an emergent construct from the brain. Secondly, there is historical fluidity around models of human constitution as inherited from a mish-mash of various historical changing worldviews. Indeed historical adoption of worldviews with regard human constitution is even visible in evolution of terms from OT to NT.[^73]

Finally, the location of the mind is particularly murky in biblical language. There is no systematic biblical definition as to what it entails, where its boundaries are drawn, nor how it might connect with the body, heart, soul or spirit.[^74] Indeed, the mind is frustratingly difficult to locate within the major models of constitution.

So the "mainline" position of dichotomism is elusive as to whether the mind is encapsulated under the "body" or under the "spirit/soul", while trichotomism's extra delineation between spirit and soul (i.e. body, spirit and soul) provides no further clarity. For example, in Michael Horton's treatment of constitution he can state 'nowhere in the Bible is the soul identified with the mind,' yet two pages later in summary of his supported position (dichotomism) he curiously writes 'human beings are composed of soul (synonymous with spirit or mind)...'[^75] Similarly the ambiguity is found between authors. With regard to trichotomism, Horton connects mind with spirit (constitution of spirit+mind, soul, body); while Erickson talks of soul as 'the psychological element, the basis of reason... '[^76] Nevertheless, in general, the mind tends to be associated with the immaterial (soul/spirit) aspect of human constitution, while the brain is associated with the body - a contrary position to the majority Cognitive Science presumptions behind of IA science.

The monism model, of course, avoids this problem. Indeed, in its unitary philosophical definition it aligns with the scientific consensus of seeing humans as physical, with concepts of mind and soul being emergent from within neurological workings of the brain. However, Christian theology has tended to struggle to reconcile monism against its (apparent) incompatibility with a post-mortem, disembodied intermediate state (2Cor.5:1-10; Phil.1:23-24, Cf. Lk.23:43), though there are exceptions.[^77]

Of course, a classification into monist, dichotomist and trichotomist positions belies the true spectrum of the (unsurprisingly?) ongoing debate. Between the two extremes of reductive materialism and radical dualism we find a plethora of models.[^78] And, with regard to the mind-body problem specifically, the jury is still out.[^79]

Locating Intelligence Amplification constitutionally within each model variant is beyond the scope of this project.[^80] Rather, it is argued that irrespective of constitution IA is better thought of, not as an additive technology external to human constitution, but rather as an extension or stretching of the boundaries of existing constitution. Moreover, this boundary-stretching can more fruitfully be envisaged under the broad categories of "material" and "immaterial" constitution. In turn, these broad categories can then find expression within the various competing models, and indeed IA offers a fresh lens for assessing the nuances between them.

So, in the material sense, machine augmented intelligence is seen as an augmentation of the biological brain with a computational machine. This position is the standard materialist (and monist) position common to Transhumanism. However, IA can also be envisaged in an immaterial sense. Instinctively this sounds bizarre given the very definition of IA includes material technology. Nevertheless, this project argues that an immaterial sense of cognitive augmentation can be understood as an extension of "self-identity" through technology. If, contra materialism, the "self" is not equivalent to my body/brain (possibly the "mind" in some dichotomist views), then the "self" also need not be equivalent to technological extensions to the body/brain; in whatever way the immaterial substance differs from biology, so too can it differ from technology.[^81] Indeed if we are already cognitive cyborgs in a "soft" sense (as this project argues) then our "self" already includes this immaterial technological dimension.[^82] Again, though the idea is confronting at first blush, if personal identity is part of "self" then it is already the product of technological influences - if "who I am" in new creation has any continuity with "who I am" today, it is inextricably already shaped by information technology.

In this way, cognitive augmentation can be considered part of the constitution of an individual - expanded and perhaps of a different kind, but the same "self" - irrespective of whether IA augments the material or immaterial.

However, a point of difference does present itself between the operations of the brain versus operations of computational hardware; not merely in the "type" of cognition,[^83] but more significantly, in location. Previously in LP1 we positioned "embodiment" as a limit in our anthropological model, and here we argue for constitutional individuality - a unitary self. Within the immanence limits of LP1 and LP2, machine cognition is said to still find compatibility when considered as a viable extension of oneself - stretching human constitution while maintaining embodiment across biological brain and computational hardware. However, unlike the biological brain, the location of this computational hardware is no longer discretely "contained" within the physical boundaries of my body, but is embodied by networked cloud infrastructure where hardware components - processing cores, network switches, storage devices - are shared by design.

Do shared elements of constitution act as a defeater against IA by denying "distinctive" individuality? We turn to examine this question now.

#### Individuality in Biblical Metanarratives

Individuality is pre-supposed and affirmed in both the restorative and teleological Biblical metanarratives (introduced in LP1). In the teleological metanarrative, at creation humans are made as discreet individuals Adam and Eve and at new creation each person is individually raised (and judged). In the restorative metanarrative, sin affects each and every individual person, with an expectation for salvation on a person-by-person basis. While the Church may have a greater or lesser role to play in various ecclesial traditions, salvation is nevertheless seen as effective for distinct individuals, requiring the repentance and faith of that particular person.

Indeed, the individual distinctiveness in Scripture is not merely circumstantial. Throughout Scripture, the naming (and renaming) of distinct individuals identifies them as unique, their name a reflection of their own particular nature. In the Old Testament, Adam is a wordplay on *adamah* ("earth") while Eve means "to live;" Abram and Sarai ("high father" and "my princess") were renamed Abraham and Sarah ("father of a multitude" and "mother of nations." Gen.17:5,15); in the New Testament, Peter is the "rock" upon which the church is build (Matt.16:18) and even Jesus's own name means "God saviour" (and Immanuel, "God with us.").

So too, familial lineage traces distinct individuals - a careful parent-child chain presented as evidence of God's people. So, Genesis explicitly traces from Adam to Noah (5:1-32) and from Noah to Abram (11:10-32), while the New Testament picks up from there tracing Jesus' lineage back through King David to Abraham (Matt.1:2-16; Lk.3:23-34). Under the new covenant, Gentiles are included through familial language of adoption as sons and daughters (Gal.4:4-5) children of God (Jn.1:12-13; 2Cor.6:18).

Finally, Scripture explicitly affirms diversity of distinct individuals (not uniformity). The New Testament describes the church as different parts of a body (1Cor.12:12-27), each performing a unique function, with distinct and different spiritual gifts (1Cor.12:4-11; 28-30. Cf. Rom.12:6-8; Eph.4:11). So also, the eschatological vision of Revelation 7:9, when the children of God from across history will ultimately gather, celebrates the diversity of God's children - with people from every nation, tribe, people and language.

Scriptures never treats humanity as a "hive" collective, but explicitly (not merely circumstantially) affirms each distinct person in their named identity, their familial status as individual sons and daughters, and through human diversity.[^84]

Against these formulations, Christianity clearly upholds individuals as distinctive and discreet. At first blush, then, the shared constitution implicit in IA computational hardware seems at odds with the biblical expectation and affirmation of personal uniqueness. How can a person remain a unique individual while part of their constitution is shared or inhabited by another?

Yet it is exactly this idea that lies at the heart of Scripture, in the rubric "Union with Christ." As before, this project seeks a theological precedent towards biblical compatibility; from spiritual "Union with Christ" towards machine union of IA.

#### Compatible Theological Precedent: Union with Christ

##### Individuality in union...

The language used in connection with Union with Christ takes many forms - Macaskill's survey of the New Testament notes 'a dizzying range of potentially conflicting ways in which this union is understood.'[^85] Nevertheless, it is inescapable that some expressions strongly frame the idea of "union" in ontological terms, as a non-metaphorical participation "in Christ" or "with Christ" - a human-divine union of a human and Christ.[^86]

Being "in" or "with" Christ is strongly represented in Paul's epistles. Particularly and powerfully in Ephesians 1:3-14 (examined earlier). The expression "in Christ" is found 39 times in the letter, with a myriad of presuppositional arrangements found in nearly every verse of his introduction: 'in Christ' (v.3, 12); 'in him' (v.4, 7, 9, 11, 13); 'through Christ' (v.5); 'in the One he loves' (v.6).[^87] Verse 10 explicitly broadens this unity to its eschatological climax - 'to bring unity to all things in heaven and on earth under Christ.'

And yet somehow, while ontologically participating in an effectively existential union, an individual maintains their unique individuality while sharing of their person with the person of Christ.

Moreover, the nature of this union is not only a sharing between the human and the divine, for it is contingent upon the humanity of Christ. Christ is incarnate as human and we are united to Him, by grace through faith, according to his humanity; in a sense "Union with Christ" acts as a human-human union:

> Not only did Christ die and rise again, but when he died, his people died to sin, and when he rose, we rose with him to new life (Rom.6:4; cf. Col.3:12-13). It is "Christ in you" who is our hope of glory (Col.1:27; cf. Rom.8:10). The doctrine of union with Christ is not only about ourselves in Christ, but also about Christ in us (2Cor.13:5; Gal.4:19).[^88]

So, this "union" is seen as necessary for the "application of redemption" - the human believer needs to be joined in the life and work of Christ. Thus, John Frame elevates "union" as the existential perspective defining who we are in Christ. For Frame, "union" with Christ existentially joins believers to Christ's offices (as prophet, priest and king) and his work (in temporal-history in his incarnation, life, death, resurrection and ascension).[^89]

##### ...restoring full expression of oneself

Indeed, Scripture reveals that it is only through this shared Christ-union, a type of "hybridity" with Christ, that human individuality finds full expression - not individuality despite union, but individuality through union, as the Holy Spirit works to shape the individual to be like who they are created to be. What was at war within the nature of the individual (Rom.7:14-25), is restored and released for those who are 'in Christ Jesus' (Rom.8:1-14).

So, in the "Union with Christ" rubric we see that discreet individuality is not mutually exclusive to all forms of shared humanity; we have, in a believer's union with Christ, an example of existential compatibility of shared nature, as the restorative centre of the gospel story.

Clearly, however, union with Christ is a unique spiritual case. As (in LP1), new bodies in new creation merely presented a unique use case for compatibility for non-fleshy bodies, but not for biblical warrant; so here, the believer's individual union with Christ provides biblical compatibility, but in itself is insufficient warrant for an anthropological inclusion of IA. Explaining the biblical warrant, will be the focus of the transcendence aspect of this limit-pair.

Before turning to this, a brief summary of the "individual limit" is in order. Individuality is a core to a Christian Anthropology, defining humans as (1) constitutionally unitary beings and (2) distinctly particular beings. Though theology has tended to study the former (in wrestling with post-mortem states), it is the latter affirmation of human particularity that has far greater and more explicit biblical attestation. This biblical emphasis has tended to be ignored given, outside of philosophy, the discreetness of human beings is an everyday presumed reality. However, the introduction of IA (amongst other transhumanist technologies) warrants closer examination of the biblical data where even this brief survey demonstrates that human discreetness is not merely a circumstantial by-product of having to talk about humans, but is explicitly affirmed and central to the teleological and restorative "backbone" of scripture.

### Transcendence Limit: Humans as Relational Persons

Individuality is affirmed in Scripture; individualism is not. Indeed, from the mid-20th century a theological anthropology has emerged that upholds "relationship" as the defining characteristic of human beings - relationship with God and relationship with one another.

So, Emil Brunner sees the "I-Thou" relationship between God and humanity as essential to the *imago dei*.[^90] For Brunner, the ontological essence of humans lies in our relationship with God.

> [the imago dei is] the "I" derived from the "Thou." Hence it cannot be understood by looking at humanity, but only by looking at God... humanity is only truly human when it is in God. Then, and then only, is humanity truly itself.[^91]

The ontological relationality of humans forms the transcendent limit of LP2. Specifically, cognitive transformation via IA remains human only when relationship - with God and with other humans - is maintained (or enhanced).

To explore this relational limit, our perspective switches direction - from looking outwards from the perspective of an individual, to looking inward a sociological perspective. So, this project uses Murray Jardine's "socio-anthro-techno-theology" as a springboard.[^92] (1) Sociologically, Jardine sees Western Liberalism as individualistic, due in part to a partially realised (post-Reformation) Christian worldview; as a result, Christian community is muted. (2) Anthropologically, humans are creative, and this creativity, based theologically in speech, is fundamentally relational; a reclamation of this fully realised Christian worldview can restore Christian community. Finally, (3) human creative capacities are defined in practice by their technological manifestations. For this project, if information communication technology are the technological manifestations of creative relational speech, then any cognitive augmentation with such technologies are profoundly relational.

#### Jardine's Sociological Observations towards Technological Relationality

Turning to look at general societal constructs of modern liberalism, Murray Jardine argues that (Western) society is existentially wrestling with the transformative power of technology:

> Present day Western societies are in the grip of a profound moral crisis, and this crisis lies in the inability of modern people to make moral sense of the human creative powers - that is the human capacities to change the world - manifested in technology.[^93]

Further, Jardine argues that liberal capitalist democracy places individual freedom as its foundational and fundamental principle, and, while in theory the maximising of individual freedom should have sidestepped (premodern) moral absolutes regarding "how people should live," the historical reality has created highly individualistic societies where communal moral conversation is effectively impossible:[^94]

> This expressive individualist culture utterly destroys any possibility of even talking about, much less practicing, community or morality, resulting in a nihilistic culture of isolated individuals engaged in obsessive work and consumption that ultimately cannot even reproduce itself and is thus doomed to extinction.[^95]

This assessment of modern liberalism is damning, and Jardine does not absolve Christianity of blame. Instead, he explicitly sees individualism as a result of an under-realised and partial biblical anthropology - a "secularised Protestantism" that is essentially a distortion of the Protestant work ethic.[^96]

Ironically, Jardine's own proposed solution can be read as similarly isolationist. He calls for the creation of "local face-to-face micro-communities" that proactively carve out the time and space to foster the "speech-dimension" of human experience.... an 'alternative to liberal capitalist democracy as it approaches its collapse.'[^97] And though, this "separatist" proposal might seem radical, Jardine's concerns regarding a modern overemphasis on individuality are legitimate; individualism is antithetical to any anthropology that sees relationality as essential to human nature. Even notwithstanding Scripture's denouncement of self-reliance and self-idolatry (pride), within a framework of relational anthropology, humans are simply not created to be alone; that is, they are less human when alone.

This conclusion finds support in the theology of both Emil Brunner (noted earlier) and Karl Barth.[^98] For Brunner, the *imago dei* lies in a ready-made "natural" point of contact for relationship with God. There therefore exists a universal responsibility to respond to God (irrespective of whether or not such a response has occurred), and a subsequent "responsibility-in-love" in our relationship with fellow humans. For Barth, human beings are similarly intended for "vertical" God-human relationship, and "horizontal" human-human relationships. Relationality is an image of God in that it reflects a God who himself is relational in the Trinity (emphasising "coinherence" or "perichoresis") and this relationality is restored by revelation by the Word of God (in Christ, through the Spirit).

Jardine's own (novel) argument for human relationality provides a helpful contribution to this study of IA. For Jardine, the act of speech has creative and relational implications for biblical anthropology. God and humans are "creative" through "speech", where "speech" is inherently communal and shapes society, and "creativity" manifests as technology. Ergo, information and communications technology - i.e. the technology of speech - plays a profoundly powerful role for shaping communal society. In a sense intelligence amplification technology acts as relational amplification.

Jardine's central idea extends from seeing God's creative process as a "dynamic" act of speech (Gen.1). Humans, who themselves have the creative capacity for speech (Cf. Gen.2:20), therefore have a part to play in this creative process. Moreover, the act of speech is implicitly communal - speech normally, and paradigmatically, involves more than one person; people don't (usually) speak to themselves.[^99]

So, (1) God is communal because he speaks (to humans and within his triune being); (2) humans are communal because we speak (to God and to one another); and, (3) this is "cosmologically intrinsic" given creation itself is an act of speaking (a "dynamic creative process").

If we follow Jardine's conception, then (4) the technical manifestation of language and speech are modern information and communication technology;[^100] and, finally (5) Intelligence Amplification directly unites us with these technologies. IA is the logical extension of Jardine's speech-centric anthropology; and if IA can amplify relationality, it amplifies our humanity.

Thus, it is possible to conceive of IA technology as relational technology. For example, the "local face-to-face micro-communities" envisaged by Jardine already rudimentarily exist in virtual reality through IA, carving out a time and (cyber)space for "alternative" shared human experiences. So, social network communities through to more fully-realised "worlds" of Second Life, Eve Online and even World of Warcraft already form relational "petri dishes" for sociologists. IA technologies are fundamentally information and communication technologies - technologies that are most directly created as relational.

#### Godfather, Part 1: Teilhard's Technological Communion

Hence, this anthropological model explicitly pairs individuality with relationality; affirming individuality whilst denouncing individualism. Prosaically yet vitally, without individuals, there is no relationship, and without relationship, there is no individual. Pursuit of IA is limited by an individual and relational limit-pair.

Seeing humans as ontologically relational is particularly pressing if, as per Jardine, individual freedom is overly emphasized in modern Western liberalism relative to historical and cultural norms.[^101] For IA amplifies prevailing value systems "warts and all;" any cultural biases risk being programmatically codified, freezing and/or amplifying prevailing value systems with limited recourse.

This "bias encoding" risk is examined in the automation limit of LP3. For now, we look to one of the "godfathers" of techno-societal theology, Pierre Cardin de Teilhard (1881-1955),[^102] as an advocate for this limit-pair's dual emphasis of both human individuality and relationality. For Teilhard a philosopher, palaeontologist, geologist and Jesuit priest[^103] - technology is the natural "next-step" in the evolution of human intelligence and, despite transient reservations, will inevitably and ultimately act towards social progress.[^104] Information and communication technologies in particular lie at the centre of this social progress.

Teilhard's speculative hope follows an evolutionary trajectory common to many secular transhumanists: an initial "Alpha Point" (the Big Bang) coalesces to form baryspheres (planets); from which emerge hydrosphere and atmosphere (life-supporting planets), and the subsequent evolution of a biosphere (ecosystem).[^105] Finally, human beings, as thinking beings, exist at the apex of this biosphere; the ongoing evolution of their collective thinking is defined by Teilhard as the noosphere - a climactic layer of thought that moves inexorably towards a "shared consciousness" akin, in its final form, to concepts of the singularity held by transhumanists in the vein of Ray Kurzweil.[^106] Communications technology connects and accelerates minds towards this increasingly shared consciousness, with the effect of multiplying and deepening relationships between people. So, even in the communication technologies of his own age - the 'network of radio and television communications,' and the 'growth of... electronic computers' - Teilhard's observes a:

> ...direct synchronisation of brains through the mysterious power of telepathy, [that] already link us in all sorts of "etherized" consciousness.[^107]

Importantly, for Teilhard, technology relationally draws people together while yet retaining their individuality. Teilhard himself uses the word "personality" as distinct from "individuality" (which he associates with individualism) and envisages an eschatological "Omega Point" of divine unification of minds between humans and God - a perfect union with God while maintaining distinct personality. For Teilhard, this is the final Christological culmination - distinct human personalities fully united with the indivisible mind of Christ.[^108]

Teilhard's radical vision of the Omega Point (though weak against the embodiment limit of LP1)[^109] supports the anthropological duality of this limit-pair: humans are individual and relational. And, building on the transformation trajectory of LP1, this project concurs with Teilhard's vision that technology can play a role even in eschatological relationship. If the modern Internet can be seen as another step in realising Teilhard's noosphere, a technological network that can increase human relationality between human individuals, then emergent IA technologies act as merely the next, albeit more invasive, step along this same path.[^110]

We turn now to suggest how theology might help pair the limits of LP2.

### Pairing the Limits: "Communion" as Individual Unity in Relational Love

In a general sense, of course, love must be the defining Christian quality of any relationship between individuals. Jesus describes love of God and love of others as the "greatest commandment" upon which hang all the Law and the Prophets (Matt.22:36-40); and the very definition of love is found in God's sending of himself in Jesus as an atoning sacrifice for humans while we were still in sin - an example that spurs and seeks fullness in our love, one for one another (1Jn.4:10-11).

Indeed, for our study, "knowledge without love" is just a clanging symbol (1Cor.13:1); love builds up while knowledge, without love, merely puffs up (1Cor.8:1). So, Micah Redding, the executive director of the Christian Transhumanist Association, similarly affirms the centrality of love in his organisation's Christian Transhumanist Affirmation.[^111] For Redding, Christianity is love, and (more controversially) Christianity is Transhumanism.[^112]

However, love is also all-encompassing in its breadth and, as such, this project seeks a narrower focus about how love might more precisely pair the individual-relational limits. It is suggested that in ecclesiology - the theology of church - more tangible direction can be found. Theologically this is the natural extension from the individual-focused "Union with Christ," presented earlier, towards the shared communion of ecclesiology - the expression of Christian love in "fellowship."

#### Implications from Union: Communion

Before beginning, it is worth noting that looking at the theology of the Church might strike the reader as a peculiar move in discussions of Intelligence Amplification. Indeed, the Church, when conceived of "institutionally" is often popularly connected with that religious tradition that seeks to thwart scientific and technological progress. The validity of this perspective lies beyond the scope of this project, but here, the church is not defined socio-historically,[^113] but rather, theologically - the church as God's people and the body of Christ. As Christology and eschatology earlier informed an embodiment-transformation anthropology (LP1), here Christology and ecclesiology informs individual-relational anthropology (LP2).

Thus, ecclesiology expresses human nature as intended to be - in communion with each other and God. Moreover, though the church is defined as the community of believers, it is argued that theological implications for human identity are universal, and legitimately inform anthropological limits for everyone.

The language of union "in Christ" and "with Christ" does not merely represent an individual's transformed nature through their own participation in Christ, but is frequently connected with the fellowship of believers' common participation in Christ. The communal dimension is inexorable: as the transformation of each member orients them towards becoming like Christ, there is inevitable drawing together of all members around this common central person.[^114]

Thus, as driven first by Bonhoeffer, and extended by Barth, the twentieth century has emphasized an ecclesiology anchored in Christology.[^115] Seeing the Church as the very body of Christ himself has played a crucial role in counterbalancing the dominant institutional understanding of the church up until that point (including the Roman Catholic Church).[^116] That this metaphor has multiple NT references grants it theological weight. The church members are the many parts of the body of Christ (1Cor.12:27-28; Rom.12:4-8), and the head of that body is Christ (Eph.5:23; Col.1:18).

The relational identity of the church is inherent in the common imagery used to describe it. The church is familial ("sons and daughters" of the "father") and societal ("citizens" of the "kingdom of God"). In the New Testament, the Old Testament "apartness" of a holy God from humanity (as represented in the tabernacle and temple) is replaced with intimate fellowship as sons and daughters of God's family (2Cor.6:16-18). The language of genealogical lineage (discussed earlier), is reinterpreted and fulfilled in Jesus "Kingdom of God" motif, the language and imagery used of the "Church" emphasises unity - with each other and, particularly, union with/in Christ. It is union with Christ (by the Spirit) - our being "in Christ" (and Christ in us) - that connects the Church to the Kingdom of God. The church is a society - citizens of the Kingdom of God, with Christ as king (Eph.2:19). Systematically, Christians are counted amongst the kingdom community, only because we are united with the Christ who is the firstfruits of that kingdom.

So, John Zizioulas writes 'there is no true being without communion... communion is an ontological category.'[^117] Tracing the Trinitarian work of the Cappadocian Fathers, he sees the church as an anticipation of the relational communion with the (social) Trinity; already experiencing an eschatological foretaste of the perfected church when joining with God in the sacrament of the Eucharist.[^118]

And so, ontologically, through Union with Christ, the Church necessarily acts towards Christ; the Holy Spirit directing them towards Christ-like "character" and "function" - even now making them fit for, and doing the work of, the new heaven and the new earth.[^119] And, as people are drawn closer to Christ, they are inevitably also drawn closer to on another. Following LP1, this Spirit-led "self-transformation" encompasses IA technologies, and now in LP2 we see that, as relational technology, IA also plays mediating role in relationship.[^120] As the Holy Spirit shapes humans to be like what they already are, this should also shape our "technological selves" - both individually and relationally.

Moreover, if Christian communion ontologically "is" (in Christ) and "is becoming" (by the Spirit) a society of love inclusive of its technological manifestation; this can act as a universal exemplar for human society at large. Thus ecclesiology, with its anthropology in communion, forms the suggested model for pairing the individual and relational limits for IA.

## Limit-Pair Three: Agency and Automation

Limit-Pair Three deals with human agency (immanence limit) and automation (transcendence limit). In LP1 we observed that technology is intrinsically and unavoidably transformative. In LP2 we observed that this transformation extended beyond the individual to also play a mediating role in relationship and, hence, society at large. In this limit-pair we consider the anthropological implications upon human will - concluding (provocatively) that those already in union with Christ, are uniquely equipped to maintain human agency, and indeed maximise the Christocentric agency of IA.

### Immanence Limit: Humans as Persons with Agency

The agency limit of LP3 rests on the biblical restorative metanarrative where human agency is both inherent in creation (and "good") but also corrupted by sin. Moreover, any technological manifestations of human agency inherit this intended-but-corrupted tension. Against Teilhard's optimism we explore Jacques Ellul's sociological observations of the enslaving effects of technology; cautioning that IA technologies in particular profoundly affect human agency as they most directly transform "self-awareness" and "free will."

#### Human Agency: Inherent and Corrupted

It currently appears to be neurologically unclear how the physical brain gives rise to our subjective experiences of things. So, within the interdisciplinary field of cognitive science, the subjective phenomenological experiences of "intentionality" (the quality of mental states - thoughts, beliefs, desires and hopes - as directed towards some object or state of being) and "qualia" (qualities or properties as subjectively perceived or experienced by a person), and also the higher order subjective phenomena of "self-awareness" and "free will" are, lacking clinical evidence, not informed by neurobiology, but by the theoretical schools of philosophy and artificial intelligence research. So, Green notes the existence of a "qualia" gap between 'the physical correlates of a phenomenal state' and 'our subjective feelings of that state.'[^121] While Graves's similarly notes:

> When scientists examine the brain closely in a variety of experiments, they learn much about perception, learning, memory, action, planning and certain other mental phenomena, but not as much about self, intentionality, or free will.[^122]

Within the context of this limit-pair, the collection of these subjective phenomena will be referred to (simplistically) as "human agency". And, while "evidence gaps" undoubtedly exist in current neurobiological research, this theological anthropology does not build its foundations upon them, but instead locates "human agency" as described biblically - specifically within the restorative metanarrative.[^123] Indeed, though the correlation between anthropological dualism and the "gap in science" is intriguing, any argument built solely upon this mind-body (mind-brain) gap—e.g. cognition in the material brain, subjective agency in the immaterial mind - is an inadequate (and potentially stopgap) solution.[^124]

So, returning to the three "technological indicators" of Genesis 2-3 (as presented in LP1), we see that "human agency" seems intrinsic alongside technology in these accounts. Firstly, Genesis 2:15 implies human agency in God's expectation to cultivate the garden-presumably, humans are (literally or figuratively) expected to apply their own volition in working the garden, planning the layout and adjusting for seasonal variation. Secondly, Genesis 2:19-20-the "first technology" - demonstrates human agency in the naming of God's animals - Adam "considers," "imagines," and "pronounces" a label befitting each creature. Finally, and foundationally for the restorative metanarrative, Genesis 3:21 demonstrates God's ongoing agency towards transforming sinful human agency in the "technological upgrading" of Adam and Eve's garments. At a minimum therefore, it can be said that agency is inherent to human beings at creation, and moreover, human agency is coupled with technology. As such, if these are technological indicators, it would seem that technological agency is inherent at creation.

However, as already explicit in the "first technological upgrade," human agency is also quickly distorted by sin. Indeed, the biblical conception of "sinful human nature" can be described as a distortion to human agency in particular (Rom.7:14-25). With sin, our subjective perception of reality is twisted and human agency is hamstrung. And yet, the restorative metanarrative of the Bible could be framed as God's ongoing transformational agency amidst human sinful agency, culminating in the work of Christ. In him, human perception and freedom is restored (Eph.5:8).

The restoration of human agency from sin's corrupting influence reinforces the earlier theological suggestions for LP1 and LP2. So, love and fellowship, which pair the individual and relational limits of LP2, can be seen as acts of restored agency towards one another (1Cor.13:4-7) as exemplified by God's agency in sending his son (1Jn.4:10). Similarly, the strong-weak inversion, presented in LP1, demonstrates Christ-like "higher order" agency of self-reflection and self-giving as expressed in mercy, compassion and grace; fuller expressions of human agency that are less reactive and more self-controlled.

Indeed, this Christian assessment of agency as moral-yet-impeded stands distinct from what Tracy Trothen sees as the normative Western conception of autonomy that underlies most transhumanist ethics, where 'the concept of autonomy is conflated with individual rights and choice' (emphasis mine).[^125] If autonomy is reduced to individual choice alone, then Intelligence Amplification biases the amplification of the cognitive capacities of "first adopter" individuals. Trothen goes on to note that societally such an individualistic approach 'ignores the uneven global power distribution' - increasing agency of some, while societally limiting the agency of others with ultimately negative spiritual consequences; in effect taking the existing "digital divide" and solidifying it in cognitive inequity across individuals.[^126] Here we can glimpse how the agency limit of LP3 intersects with the relational-love anthropology of LP2 and the weak-is-strong navigational proposal of LP1. Without restoration, human agency is selfish.

#### Godfather, Part 2: Ellul's Technological Enslavement

The observation that technology sometimes reduces human agency is taken to its existential conclusion in the work of Jacques Ellul. While this second "godfather" of techno-societal theology, like Teilhard (and this author), agrees that technology is unavoidably transformative, he sees technological transformation running in opposition to Teilhard. Where Teilhard traces an "optimistic" trajectory through a (metaphysical) evolutionary progress, Ellul's real-world observations of sociological conditions reaches a contradictory "pessimistic" conclusion - technology is not drawing people together; it is assimilating them.[^127]

For Ellul, technology is defined more narrowly as the "machine component" within what he more broadly defines as "technique". He writes (emphasis his own):

> In our technological society, technique is the totality of methods rationally arrived at and having absolute efficiency (in a given stage of development) in every field of human activity.[^128]

For Ellul, the ultimate goal of all-encompassing technique is all-encompassing efficiency; where "progress" becomes the systematic maximisation of convenience, productivity and speed. Human beings are progressively enslaved by an increasingly technological society. In direct counterpoint to Teilhard's positive vision of flourishing relational human-divine union, Ellul sees any technology-mediated union as a move towards total mental acquiescence and homogeneity:

> The conditions for psychological efficiency are, first, group integration and second, group unanimity... When psychological techniques, in close cooperation with material techniques, have at last succeeded in creating unity, all possible diversity will have disappeared and the human race will have become a bloc of complete and irrational solidarity.[^129]

Moreover, he sees these technological effects as self-reinforcing. As a technological society increasingly absorbs independent human agency, all attempted solutions towards thwarting this decreasing agency are themselves technologically mediated - technique has limited human thinking to only consider technological-oriented solutions:

> They want to restore man's lost unity, and patch together that which technical advances have separated. But only one way to accomplish this ever occurs to them, and that is to use technical means.[^130]

Directly contrasting Teilhard's advocacy of radio and television, Ellul instead pre-empts what has been coined today as "information overload;" amplifying information (in quantity and volume) and mediating communication (through the technological medium) to drown out and self-deny personal experience, judgement and thought. In the end, Ellul's hope for salvation can only come from outside - 'only God can save us from the sociological determinism that has been instituted by technology.'[^131]

Ellul's sentiments resonate with modern mainstream concerns regarding the detrimental effects of the Internet. For example, Nicholas Carrs' *The Shallows* explores how the internet might be neurologically reducing our capacity for concentration, contemplation, and reflection;[^132] and Sherry Turkle's *Alone Together* notes that social media (e.g. Facebook) that promises closeness might actually impair our relationships, leaving us less connected to one another.[^133]

Thus, even though today's generally available IA does not as yet appear to exhibit much independent machine agency (discussed next), these technologies nevertheless already impose a mediating effect upon existing human agency. And again, this impact to human agency is particularly profound with IA technology. For no other technology so directly affects the mind with the potential (particularly in its more speculative "hard IA" forms) to change human will directly and, indeed, recursively. As Cole-Turner asks: how are we to think of "self" that modifies itself?

> But now, as we turn technology on ourselves so that we change our own bodies and brains, the "I" is swept up in the change and modified through its own action... blurring the lines between subject and object, agent and effect. When "I" use technology to change myself, just who is this "I" who decides and who is the "I" that is the result of the decision?[^134]

Before addressing this conundrum, a brief summary of the agency limit of LP3 is helpful: (1) Humans are biblically affirmed as persons with agency, where human agency is inherently created "good," but also corrupted by sin; (2) technology is a manifestation of that (good and bad) agency, but also reciprocally affects and transforms that same human agency; and, (3) IA technologies most profoundly affects human agency in ways that are direct and recursive.

Theologically then, in this agency limit of LP3, IA increases two main anthropological risks: firstly, IA can and, as observed, sometimes does reduce human agency (and by extension our humanity); secondly, and perhaps paradoxically, IA amplifies human agency - both the inherent good and sin's corruption.

We turn now to the transcendence limit before treating these risks in LP3's conclusion.

### Transcendence Limit: Humans as Persons that Automate

Of the anthropological limits proposed thus far, the "automation" limit seems most alien to traditional Christian theology. Yet it is argued that automation - in this project defined as the efficiencies gained from technology, particularly information technologies - are intrinsic (though not emphatic) in the Biblical narrative and evident through the history of the church. Against Ellul, technological efficiency is therefore not in itself "naturally" destructive to human nature. Indeed, seeing humans as self-automating beings is a natural progression from the conception of humans as ontologically technological transformative. Humans as technological agents are predisposed towards efficiency; as a limit-pair, humans are self-autonomous and self-automating.

Examining the "automation" limit naturally moves this study towards the cutting-edge of cognitive automation - the rapidly growing field of augmentation via Artificial Intelligence agents; that is, augmented thinking via increasingly powerful self-learning algorithms (IA via AI). Where the transcendence limits of LP1 and LP2 dealt primarily with "transformative" implications of IA, the transcendence limit of LP3 engages the anthropological implications of cognitive "amplification."

#### Automation as Human Technological Efficiency

Up front, it is worth noting that emphasis on technological efficiency is difficult given "theologies of efficiency" in general are not forthcoming. Indeed, often "efficiency" is negatively perceived in theology.[^135] One helpful exception comes from Michael E. Cafferky, whose *Biblical Theology of (economic) Efficiency* argues that:

> the Bible encourages the pursuit of operational efficiency within the constraints of moral principles designed to foster abundant living in all dimensions considered important in the Bible: social, physical, spiritual, political, and economic[^136]

Though direct evidence is circumstantial, it plays an implicit consideration under the doctrine of stewardship - wisdom to use our gifts well. At a minimum, technological efficiency is inextricably involved in the ongoing story of the gospel and the church. As noted in LP1, "good" technology is often unnoticed throughout Scripture. Technology functions as tools that enable efficiencies and, in the biblical context, these efficiencies are often instrumental in God's plans.

So, the technology of Noah's Ark plays an instrumental role in saving human and animal life in the mini-recreation of the flood; amidst oral tradition, the technology of writing codified the Mosaic Law for efficient and just religious and civil practice;[^137] and blueprints, measurements and construction of the tabernacle and temple are early examples of the affinity between technology and art, where "craftsmanship" enhances aesthetic and religious symbolism for worship.

More prosaically, though no less importantly, technological efficiencies have played an intrinsic part in gospel missions. Within the New Testament, ancient transportation infrastructure of roads and ships seem to mark out efficient routes for Paul's church planting missions, while continued technological advancements in infrastructure and logistics coincide with continued outward gospel expansion through history. The printing press was a technological catalyst in the Reformation; radio broadcasts across national borders; the Jesus Film has been seen around the world; and the digital revolution enables "on-demand" access to vast theological libraries - ancient and new, text and multi-media. Sermons, lectures, essays and blogs can be telecast, podcast, posted and "shared," with ongoing discussion spanning time-zones and continents.[^138]

Indeed, a full survey of technological efficiency in the bible or church history is a fraught exercise not due to the paucity of the data, but due to the implicit embedding of technology that pervades society and culture. In the Bible we stretch ourselves to locate "big ticket" technologies - the Ark, written word, schematics - but can fail to recognise the myriad mundane technologies that are nevertheless critical to the gospel story - musical instruments of the Psalms, measuring scales for fair trade, oxen yokes used in parable imagery, parchment for writing epistles, drinking vessel in the last supper... even the cross of Christ.

Technology, by definition, is for human efficiency and it is intricately interwoven amidst the biblical story. Technology, as instruments of efficiency, already act as catalysts and amplifiers of human agency; and this understanding is critical for ongoing engagement as technology advancement continues to accelerate.

Acutely, in the information age, technological efficiency is manifested as informational efficiency - defined here as "automation," and manifest powerfully in emerging Artificial Intelligence.

#### AI as Knowledge Automation

In the field of Intelligence Amplification, the domain of "automation" is seeing rapid convergence towards Artificial Intelligence (i.e. IA using AI). Real world examples include the emerging technologies of smart home devices, the "internet of things," big data, machine learning, and digital/cognitive assistants. All indicators point towards cognitive augmentation via AI being an inevitable near-term milestone on the journey from current "soft IA" towards more speculative "hard IA." As the power and complexity of AI increases at an accelerating rate, there is increasing anthropological (and societal) concern about how these AI agents might erode human agency - how is human agency maintained when augmented with, and potentially outgunned by, increasingly powerful AI automation?

Indeed, in recent years, voices from within the Artificial Intelligence research community have increasingly mirrored these existential concerns. For example, in 2015, experts wrote an open letter citing the risks of AI research where techniques were solely 'neutral with respect to purpose,' and highlighting the need for AI to instead be directed towards 'maximising social benefit.'[^139] In their expert view, there is a pressing need for guidance from other disciplines - economics, law, philosophy (and, as argued here, theology) - given the real risk of an imminent "intelligence explosion." If AI can redesign itself through recursive and exponential self-improvement, then even though the jump from today's "Artificial Narrow Intelligence" (ANI, suited to specific tasks) to "Artificial General Intelligence" (AGI, exhibiting human-equivalent intelligence) might take decades, the subsequent jump from AGI to "Artificial Super Intelligence" (ASI) might be much shorter - with estimates ranging from months to hours - and, depending on its (self-)programming, ASI may have little regard for human life, nor human morality.[^140]

Within AI research, "machine ethics," seeks to mitigate against "malevolent" AI (or, more accurately, "human-indifferent" AI) by creating "moral" AI - fighting fire with fire.[^141] So, a strategy towards engineering this outcome is through a kind of machine "moral-bootstrapping," where the moral agency of AI is itself directed by Artificial Moral Agents (AMAs). For example, Eliezer Yudkowsky wants to make a "friendly AI" that is safe, useful and reliably implements human values. His CEV model presents an ASI that would implement a "Coherent Extrapolated Volition" of humankind:

> In poetic terms, our coherent extrapolated volition is our wish if we knew more, thought faster, were more the people we wished we were, had grown up farther together; where the extrapolation converges rather than diverges, where our wishes cohere rather than interfere; extrapolated as we wish that extrapolated, interpreted as we wish that interpreted.[^142]

In essence, a CEV model AI seeks to make choices based on a statistical extrapolation of collective human moral values, that places weight on coherence (or "agreement") of those values across humanity (or, alternatively, to choose inaction should extrapolated coherence results fall below threshold); or, more pithily, "what the majority would want with perfect foresight."[^143]

Such efforts to direct AI, like the example of Yudkowsky's CEV model, are commendable and important to capture good morality for instrumental utility. However, within Christianity's doctrine of sin, any codification from human values is ultimately untenable; this side of new creation any coherent extrapolation inherits sin's corruptive influence over human volition.[^144] That is, fundamental to Christianity, all humans are corrupted by sin (all except one).

#### IA as Directed Automation

One fundamental challenge of "machine ethics" comes from recognising AI as separate and "other" from humans. Yudkowsky himself argues that AI should not be anthropomorphised, but seen as a fundamentally "alien" intelligence. This predisposes paradigms of AI expressed in duality - human versus AI, us or them, friend or foe. Here, this study of human extension offers a helpful shift in perspective - seeing AI as extension of "self," rather than external to self. Indeed, perhaps unsurprisingly, Yudkowsky's own argument for how humans might effectively program a Friendly AI helps to support this study of anthropological extension.

He posits that the only way for a human programmer to effectively "seed" an alien intelligence to become human-friendly, is to try to become a Friendly AI themselves:[^145]

> In a sense, the only way to create a Friendly AI... is to try and become a Friendly AI yourself, so that you will contain the internally coherent functional complexity that you need to pass on to the Friendly AI.

Ultimately for the programmer, the objective is:

> not to achieve unity of purpose between yourself and the Friendly AI; the objective is to achieve unity of purpose between an idealized version of yourself and the Friendly AI. Or, better yet, unity between the Friendly AI and an idealized altruistic human.

He himself realises that the notion sounds 'a little mystical,' requiring a 'complete change of cognitive architecture,' and that the burden is on the Friendly AI programmer:

> to achieve nonanthropomorphic thinking in his or her own mind so that he or she can understand and create a nonanthropomorphic Friendly AI.[^146]

Applying Yudkowsky's human-machine programming proposal with our anthropological automation limit, the trajectory of AI automation is therefore contingent on morality of the programmer. More specifically in the proposed union between human programmer and machine, the trajectory is not merely contingent upon morality in their actions, but in their human nature. AI morality is not merely an ethical concern, but an ontological one.

Theologically then, where humans are slaves to sin, technological automation makes efficient that enslavement (as per Ellul's technique) (Rom.6:20); but, where humans are no longer slaves to sin but slaves to Christ, technological automation makes efficient that enslavement also - where, of course, being "slaves to Christ" paradoxically describes humanity's true freedom to be who they were made to be into eternity (Rom.6:22).

Indeed, the theological parallels in Yudkowsky's proposal are striking.[^147] For Yudkowsky, humans joining with machines imparts human values to machines (and humans have the experience of being a machine); biblically, God joining with humans imparts God's values to humans (and God, in Christ, has the experience of being human). So, 1 Corinthians 2:15-16 states:

> The person with the Spirit makes judgments about all things, but such a person is not subject to merely human judgments, for, "Who has known the mind of the Lord so as to instruct him?" But we have the mind of Christ.

Repeating the "friendly AI" language of Yudkowsky, Christians are already "mystically" united with Christ who is the "idealized altruistic human," through whom we have received a "complete change of cognitive architecture" in sharing the mind of Christ; a mind that is at once "human-friendly" in Christ's humanity and "alien" in Christ's all-surpassing divinity. Theological language is more ancient, but if the mind of Christ directs and transforms human minds, then there is no better union for directing and transforming artificial minds.[^148]

This brings us to the more speculative proposal of this anthropology - an anthropology that sees humans as Christocentric Self-Automating Agents.

### Pairing the Limits: Agents for Christocentric Automation

Though all technology plays a mediating role, this is particularly the case for IA technologies that play an increasingly direct mediation on human thinking and human will. This is exacerbated further (potentially exponentially) as humanity moves towards augmentation via external artificial intelligence agents; it is critical that automation via machine agency does not erode human agency (the agency limit).

Yet, against "conservative" approaches that seek to simply "block" technology, it is argued that, theologically, humans are intrinsically technological efficient beings where efficiency is not the goal, but an ontological characteristic. For the information and computing technology underpinning IA, this technological efficiency largely takes the form of cognitive "automation" (the automation limit) - of which AI agents are the latest emerging type.

Therefore, in pairing these two limits, this study reaches the conclusion that Christians - described theologically as transformed by Christ - are anthropologically uniquely suited for Intelligence Amplification. In Christian theology, the divine mind and agency transforms human minds and agency, through Christ who is the apex of human and divine mind and agency. Therefore, above just "using" Intelligence Amplification, the Christian "God-transformed" mind and agency is uniquely suited to join with and direct machine minds and machine agency. In this way both agency and automation are enlarged towards Christ.

Importantly, to say Christians are uniquely suited is a theological statement of Christian Anthropology - what a Christian is - and not a manifesto championing industry triumphalism nor exclusivity. Indeed to be transformed by Christ is dependent on a once-and-ongoing revelation of our own sinfulness and fallibility. Similarly, claims of unique suitability are not claims for exclusivity. The biblical doctrine of Common Grace affirms that God works through all people - all made in his image - for genuine moral insight and custodial authority to govern and expand technology. Being uniquely suited is therefore to have a humble (LP1) and relational (LP2) posture, whilst playing a distinctive regenerative role characterised by who Christians - but only Christians - are in Christ (Matt 5:13-15).

## Conclusion

Three limit-pairs have been presented. Limit-Pair One - embodiment and transformation - laid the foundations that humans are created as technologically self-transformative; Limit-Pair Two - individuality and relationality - argued that human individuals can fully be who they are meant to be only in relational communion, and IA technology can both shape or be shaped by this union; finally Limit-Pair Three - agency and automation - concluded that, as IA automation converges with Artificial Intelligence, those in union with Christ are uniquely suited to "maintain" human agency and indeed "maximise" Christocentric agency through IA.

This anthropology is intended as a universal model for all people - a vision of humanity intended for all. Yet it remains contingent upon Christ as to how this anthropological intention is realised for any particular human being. This contingency reflects Christian anthropology generally - humans universally hold intrinsic worth as created in God's image, but are dependent upon Christ for restoration from sin's corrupting influence.

Finally, in closing, while this study has focused narrowly on IA, it is hoped that contours of broader technological themes are revealed in sharper relief: Firstly, that this study of cognitive augmentation might help raise theological consideration of technology generally, as an intrinsic part of "self." Secondly, cognitive augmentation demonstrates technology's general transformative effect upon human identity in a way that can be less noticeable with "physical" technologies. Lastly, it is hoped that consideration of the speculative cognitive augmentation of tomorrow, helps the reader to better recognise existing cognitive augmentation of today. Technology is part of humans; technology transforms humans; and, this is not new.
